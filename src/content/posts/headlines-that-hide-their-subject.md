I had fourteen articles on this site and a nagging feeling the titles weren't earning their clicks. Not that they were *bad* exactly — I'd written every one carefully. But reading the [index](https://www.smartdisruptions.com/content) as a stranger would, I often couldn't tell what a piece was actually about.

So I audited all fourteen at once. Six failed. Every single one failed the same way, which is the part worth your time — because the same defect explains two complaints I'd been treating as opposites.

## The defect: the hidden noun

Here are four of the six, as they were:

- I built my friend's food truck site with AI — **here's what actually happened**
- I stopped re-explaining myself to my AI — **here's the one file that did it**
- My AI built the whole thing — then handed me back **the list of what it couldn't do**
- I was about to publish an article about my dashboard. **First I checked whether it was safe.**

Look at what each one does. It walks right up to the interesting thing and then declines to say it. What happened? Which file? What was on the list? Was it safe?

I did that on purpose. I thought I was creating curiosity.

## Why that's one problem and not two

I'd been carrying two separate complaints about my own headlines: some felt vague, and I was afraid that fixing them would tip into sounding like clickbait. That framing had me stuck, because it makes clarity and pull into a trade — be clearer and you're duller, be punchier and you're gross.

They're the same defect wearing two faces.

**Withholding the subject to build suspense is exactly what makes a headline feel like bait.** It's the "you won't believe what happened next" move in a nicer outfit. And **withholding the subject is also exactly what makes a headline unclear**, because the subject is the thing that would have told you what the article is.

So putting the noun back fixes both at once. There's no trade. That was the whole finding:

> *First I checked whether it was safe.*

became

> *Before writing about my dashboard, I checked it. **My private task list was public.***

Clearer — and far more clickable. The specific thing was the hook the entire time; I'd been hiding my own best material to manufacture suspense about it.

## The evidence that stung

Every article here has a generated picture, and that picture carries its own short line of text — written separately from the title.

Three of the six failing articles already had a picture saying the clearer thing:

| the title said | the picture said |
|---|---|
| First I checked whether it was safe. | I assumed my task list was private. The URL returned 200 OK. |
| I gave my net-worth tracker a memory… | It looked like it needed a server. It needed a text file. |
| …then took it apart and built it again | I built the monitor inside the thing it was monitoring. |

The thinking was never the problem. I knew what each piece was about — I'd said it plainly six inches lower on the same page. The headline was the one place I got shy.

## The three tests

Run these in order. Any headline that fails one gets rewritten, not shipped.

### 1. Name the thing

**What:** the specific noun the piece is about is in the headline — the file, the finding, the number, the mistake.

**How:** ask what the most interesting concrete thing in the piece is, then check whether it's in the title. If it isn't, ask yourself why. If the answer is "I'm saving it," that's the bug, not the technique.

**Why:** suspense is what makes a headline feel like bait. The specific is what makes it feel like a person who actually did the thing. A stranger should be able to say what your piece is about in their own words after reading the title alone.

### 2. Say what the reader gets

**What:** a method, a number, a result, or a warning.

**How:** *"here's what actually happened"* is the *shape* of a promise with nothing in it. *"here are the six prompts"* is a promise. One of my headlines already did this right — I just hadn't noticed it was the rule.

**Why:** people don't click because they're curious in the abstract. They click because they can tell there's something in it for them.

### 3. Promise nothing you don't deliver

**What:** the ceiling. If the headline implies more than the piece contains, the headline is wrong.

**How:** read the title, then read the article, and ask whether you'd feel slightly cheated.

**Why:** this is the entire anti-spam rule, and it costs nothing once the first two tests pass — because a headline built from a real specific can't overpromise. It's already describing something that exists. Overpromising is what you do when you don't have a specific.

## All six, before and after

| was | now |
|---|---|
| …here's what actually happened | I built my friend's food truck a full ordering system. I shipped the simple version. |
| First I checked whether it was safe. | Before writing about my dashboard, I checked it. My private task list was public. |
| …here's the one file that did it | I stopped re-explaining myself to my AI. The fix was one file I write by hand. |
| …the list of what it couldn't do | My AI built the whole thing. It handed back the keys, the money, and the final yes. |
| I gave my net-worth tracker a memory… | My net-worth tracker runs daily with no server. I moved it without missing a day. |
| …then took it apart and built it again | I built my site's dashboard inside my site — so it went down whenever the site did. |

Eight headlines passed and I left them alone. That's worth saying, because an audit that finds everything wrong is usually the auditor showing off.

## Two things that made this cheap

**The title and the web address are separate.** I'd half-assumed changing a headline would break links, so I'd never seriously considered it. It doesn't: the address is its own field, so every existing link, share and search result still works. No redirects, nothing to clean up. Six one-line changes.

**And a near-miss, because it nearly undid itself.** My site publishes from a second copy of the content that gets promoted to the live site periodically. That copy still had the *old* titles. If I'd walked away after publishing the fix, the next promotion would have quietly put all six back — a change that reverts itself is worse than one you never made, because you stop watching. Syncing the two copies was the actual last step, not the merge.

## Try this tonight

Take the last thing you titled — a post, a proposal, an email subject line, a slide.

Ask one question: **what's the most specific thing in here, and is it in the title?**

If it isn't, and you left it out to make people curious, put it back and read both versions side by side. In all six of mine, the honest one was also the one I'd rather click.
