I set myself a goal: get a blog live and publish three posts, each one from something I'd actually built, and share every one of them somewhere real people are.

I did all of it. Blog live, three posts up, all three shared.

Then I wrote the sentence I could easily have skipped: **zero followers, no conversations started, so "shared" reached almost nobody.**

I want to talk about why saying that mattered more than the three posts did.

## What "done" looked like

The goal was written carefully, which turned out to be both good and bad.

- Blog live at a real address ✓
- Three posts, each from real work ✓
- Each one shared where real people could see it ✓

Every box, honestly ticked. I did the work. The posts are good — they're about things I actually built, with the real numbers and the parts that went wrong.

And the goal was still, in the way that counts, not met.

## The gap between shared and seen

I posted the links from a profile with no followers, no history of commenting, no relationships with anyone writing about the same things.

That's not distribution. That's putting a note in a bottle and recording that the bottle left your hand.

The number I'd been quietly using as a proxy — *did I share it?* — turned out to measure my own activity, not anything about a reader. It's a real number, and it was answering a different question than I thought.

**Publishing is the ten percent.** It's the part with a clear finish line, which is exactly why it feels like the whole job. The other ninety is showing up where the conversation already happens, repeatedly, before you have anything to link.

I knew that. I'd have said it out loud if you'd asked. I'd built the entire goal around the half I was already comfortable with.

## Why I wrote it down instead of moving on

Nothing forced me to record the zero. I'd hit every criterion. I could have written "3/3 shared ✓" and started the next thing.

Two reasons I didn't.

**First: the notes are the plan.** I keep working notes that I actually reread and that the tools I build read too. If those say "distribution: done," then the next decision gets made on top of a false floor. A month later I'd be wondering why nothing was working, with a document telling me it was.

**Second: the honest version tells you what to do next.** "3/3 shared" suggests writing post four. "Shared, but nobody saw it" points at something completely different — go be a person in the places these readers already are.

One of those is a next step. The other is momentum with no direction.

## The part I'd have got wrong on my own

I'd have written more posts.

It's the obvious move, it's the thing I'm good at, and it produces visible output every time. Four posts feels better than three.

But if nobody's reading, post four gets exactly the same reach as posts one through three, and now I've spent another week. **More of the thing that isn't working isn't a strategy.**

The bottleneck was never production. It took writing the uncomfortable sentence to see that, because "I published three posts" is such a satisfying thing to say that it hides the question of whether anyone read them.

## Try this today

If you're building something in public — a blog, a product, a portfolio:

1. **Write down what you'd have to admit to make your progress note honest.** Not "what's left" — what's *true* that you've been leaving out. Mine was one clause: *and nobody saw them*.
2. **Check whether your metric measures you or measures a reader.** "Posts published", "features shipped", "applications sent" — all yours. None of them are evidence anyone was on the other end.
3. **Ask what the honest version says to do next.** If it points somewhere different from what you were about to do, that difference is the whole value.
4. **Then do the uncomfortable half first**, while the honesty is fresh. Mine is talking to people before I have anything to promote, which is markedly less fun than writing.

Step 1 is free and takes two minutes. It's the highest-return thing in this post.

## What I'd tell myself two years ago

I used to think being honest in my own notes was a nice-to-have — a bit of self-awareness, good character, not really *work*.

It's load-bearing. Every decision I make next is built on what those notes say. A note that flatters me doesn't just feel nice; it actively sends me somewhere wrong, and it does it quietly, for as long as I keep believing it.

The three posts took weeks. The sentence that made them useful took ten seconds and cost nothing but the small discomfort of writing *zero* next to something I was proud of.

I'm still at the start of the hard half. That's the honest status, and it's a better place to stand than the version where I was finished.
